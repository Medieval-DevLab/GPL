# GPL genre direction: diegetic business simulation

> Historical reference, superseded on 22 September 2026 by [Complete game backlog](COMPLETE-GAME-BACKLOG.md), [content coverage](GAME-CONTENT-COVERAGE.md) and [desktop/photo specification](GAME-PHOTO-AND-SCREEN-SPEC.md). Older approval labels, mobile compositions, illustration-only cast, prediction gates and conflicting screen requirements below are not implementation authority.

## What went wrong in the previous attempts

The previous mockups failed for four reasons:

1. **Enterprise UI replaced game space.** Panels, rails, badges, and action bars made the
   product read like a SaaS dashboard.
2. **Professional was confused with sterile.** Accenture employees need credibility, not
   white-card minimalism.
3. **Game signals were removed instead of translated.** The earlier fantasy attempt had
   stronger spatial progression and authored atmosphere, but the fantasy world was wrong for
   the audience. The answer is to keep the game grammar and move it into a believable business
   world.
4. **The visuals were generated before the core interaction was understood.** We need to
   design the player’s physical relationship with evidence, people, and commitments first.

## Research conclusion

The strongest relevant games do not present a business dashboard:

- **80 Days** makes the route itself the world. The map is not a navigation utility; it is
  the player’s sense of place, choice, and progress.
- **Reigns** reduces the action vocabulary to a tiny gesture, then makes the world respond
  through delayed consequences.
- **Papers, Please** makes the interface objects part of the job: the rulebook, documents,
  stamps, and booth are simultaneously UI and world.
- **Mini Metro** uses a very small visual vocabulary, but the network visibly changes under
  pressure. The player manipulates a living system rather than filling fields.

The relevant design principle is **diegetic simulation UI**: information should appear as
objects, spaces, people, and changes inside the world whenever possible. Research on these
games similarly warns that extra reminders and superfluous UI can break the sense of being in a
world. [Game Developer analysis](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/-t-m-i-preserving-the-suspension-of-disbelief-through-ambiguity)

## Recommended style

### Desktop-first diegetic narrative simulation

GPL should look like a premium illustrated desktop simulation about a real client engagement.
The closest useful reference family is visual-novel presentation plus strategy-game state:
large scene art, expressive people, contextual overlays, authored events, and a small number
of persistent systems. The desktop canvas gives the game room for a full scene and readable
supporting information at the same time.

GPL should have:

- a richly composed engagement room, not a web dashboard;
- physical and digital business artefacts placed in space;
- illustrated stakeholder portraits and authored reactions;
- route progression shown through places in the engagement, not generic numbered nodes;
- limited but expressive motion: paper slides, a message arrives, a relationship thread changes,
  a document gets redlined, a route opens;
- information revealed through inspection and focus, not simultaneous panel overload;
- professional editorial typography and restrained colour, but with texture, depth, and visual
  events.

The visual reference pattern is closer to a desktop visual novel or narrative strategy game
than to a learning portal. Behance’s visual-novel work shows how a single illustrated scene,
character framing, and contextual controls can carry emotion and information together; serious
games such as *Papers, Please* make the tools of the job part of the play space. [Behance visual
novel reference](https://www.behance.net/gallery/118986345/Visual-Novel-Mockup) · [Papers,
Please / 80 Days interface analysis](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/-t-m-i-preserving-the-suspension-of-disbelief-through-ambiguity)

The world is believable business reality with game clarity. It can use an illustrated,
slightly cinematic treatment without becoming fantasy, cartoonish, or corporate stock art.

## Screen grammar under this direction

### 1. Journey map: the engagement as a place

An illustrated route through five real engagement spaces: first contact, qualification room,
shaping workshop, mobilisation room, and delivery handover. The route is a scene with people,
rooms, objects, and atmosphere; it is not a line of circles on a dark background. Completed
spaces show evidence of the player’s path; the current space is active; future spaces are
atmospheric previews.

### 2. Briefing: the dossier on the table

A chapter opens as a physical briefing pack: cover page, client photograph, marked evidence,
and one advisor note. The learner opens the pack and turns through only the evidence needed for
the next decision.

### 3. Messages and chat: communications as events

Email, chat, call transcript, and news arrive as authored moments. They are not a generic
messaging component repeated everywhere. A message can add a pin to the evidence wall, change a
stakeholder portrait, or alter the available route.

### 4. Audit: zooming through the account

The audit screen is a spatial desk: documents, people, timeline, and open questions are placed
around the client problem. The player can inspect, pin, compare, and mark an assumption. It
should feel closer to a detective board or project room than a reporting dashboard.

### 5. Decision: the commitment table

The decision screen is a large illustrated scene: a meeting room or project studio with the
client brief in the foreground, stakeholder portraits or video tiles in the scene, and three
approach artefacts presented as authored proposal sheets. Selecting an approach physically
brings it forward, highlights the relevant person and constraint, and changes the annotations in
the room. The player reviews the commitment, then makes one clear action.

There are no prediction questions for Winnability, Profitability, or Deliverability.

### 6. Consequence: the room responds

Results should not be a green panel with numbers. The chosen document is returned with comments,
a stakeholder appears with a reaction, a calendar or delivery plan changes, and an advisor
explains the principle. Numbers may support the explanation, but cannot be the scene.

## Non-negotiable anti-patterns

- no generic SaaS dashboard shell;
- no permanent left rail / centre panel / right rail on every screen;
- no flat white-card grid as the visual foundation;
- no fantasy world, children’s-game styling, or toy-like reward economy;
- no decorative “game” elements disconnected from the business state;
- no AI-generated scene used as a substitute for an interaction design;
- no screen approved without showing what the learner touches, reads, changes, and carries.

## Next visual deliverable

The next approval set must be three actual screen mockups in this direction:

1. an illustrated journey map with real engagement spaces;
2. an evidence/audit desk with inspectable business artefacts;
3. a commitment table decision screen where the selected approach visibly changes the room.

Only after those are approved should we expand to chat, cutscenes, results, and chapter
debriefs.
