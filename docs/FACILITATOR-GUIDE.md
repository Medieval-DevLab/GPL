# Facilitating GPL: the four-act game

**Status.** For version 2.0, the four-act game. The 18-decision version's guide is kept as
`FACILITATOR-GUIDE-1x.md`. Nothing here has been tested with a cohort yet: the timings are
estimates from the research behind the design, not measurements.

## What learners take away

One sentence, which the game repeats word for word:

> **Every promise that helps you win the work is a cost someone pays later — so choose your
> promises on purpose.**

It comes with four ideas, one per act, also used word for word:

| Act | The idea | What the player does |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Understand before you offer.** | Finds out what is really wrong at Orion, then opens the first meeting |
| 2 | **Not every deal is worth winning.** | Decides how much of the team to bet, then answers a rival's flashy demo |
| 3 | **Trade, don't give.** | Writes the offer, then answers Orion's buyer on price |
| 4 | **Promise only what your team can deliver.** | Decides whether to sign, then handles month five, when every promise comes due |

Learners need no sales or marketing background. The game introduces the firm (Northgate), how
a deal works, and the three bars (Win, Worth, Deliver) before the first decision.

## Session plan

The evidence on serious games is consistent: learning comes from the debrief and from more
than one sitting, not from a single long play. Wouters et al. 2013 and Clark et al. 2016 found
single sessions taught little; Tannenbaum & Cerasoli 2013 found debriefs improved performance
by about a fifth. So run it in two sittings, one to three days apart.

| Sitting | Play | Debrief | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Introduction, acts 1 and 2 (about 20 minutes) | 10 minutes | 30 minutes |
| 2 | Three recall questions, then acts 3 and 4 (about 20 minutes) | 15 minutes | 35–40 minutes |

Progress saves in the browser. A run code restores committed decisions on another device.

If you only have one session, play straight through (about 40 minutes) and keep at least 15
minutes for the debrief. Do not cut the debrief to make time.

**Recall questions to open sitting 2.** Ask before anyone plays. Let people answer from memory,
then reveal.
1. What did Sarah's trust in us depend on? *(What we knew about her business, and whether she
   could see what it rested on.)*
2. When is walking away a good decision? *(When the deal won't pay, or can't be delivered.)*
3. Name one thing your team found in act 1 that mattered later. *(Answers vary. Good ones name
   the complaint figures or Marcus Reed.)*

## Running each act's debrief

The game ends each act with its own short debrief, which shows the cause-and-effect map and
asks the player to guess which choice will come back. In a group, run the same four steps
aloud, in this order:

1. **What happened?** Two or three people say how their act ended.
2. **Guess the cause.** Before anyone explains, everyone writes down the one choice they think
   caused it. Guessing first is what makes the explanation stick (the generation effect), so
   don't skip it.
3. **See the chain.** People compare their cause-and-effect maps. Point out runs that ended
   differently because of one early choice.
4. **Where does this happen at work?** One concrete example per table. Keep the act's idea on
   the screen, in its exact words.

### Prompts for each act

**Act 1 · Understand before you offer**
- "Who did you talk to in week one, and what did that let you say on Thursday?"
- "Sarah asked about the shops. What was really wrong? How did you find out, or why didn't you?"
- At work: "When did a client brief say one thing while the real problem was elsewhere?"

**Act 2 · Not every deal is worth winning**
- "How much of the team did you bet on Orion before it had agreed to pay for anything?"
- "The rival showed off screens in the shops. Did you chase their deal or yours, and why?"
- At work: "What is the cost of chasing a deal we should have walked away from?"

**Act 3 · Trade, don't give**
- "Which promises went into your offer? Which of them will someone have to keep later?"
- "When Declan pushed on price, what did you get back for what you gave?"
- At work: "Name a discount or extra that we gave without getting anything in return."

**Act 4 · Promise only what your team can deliver**
- "Look at your promise calendar. Which promise broke, and which earlier choice broke it?"
- "In month five, did you tell Sarah, staff up, or keep quiet? What did each cost?"
- At work: "Who should check a promise before we make it?"

## Don't give the answers away

- Don't say which setting is best. Every setting has a real upside and a real cost, and many
  only pay off in combination with earlier choices.
- When someone asks "what should I pick?", answer with the act's question, not its idea.
- Compare runs, not scores. The bars are the state of the deal, not a grade, and the game
  sends no score to the LMS.

## Reading the endings

Each ending names the decisions that caused it:

| Ending | What it usually means | A question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| **Orion chose the cheaper firm** | The offer gave Declan nothing to compare except price | "What could Declan have written down to justify choosing us?" |
| **We walked away** | The deal would not have paid, or would have broken promises | "Was walking away the right call? What did it protect?" |
| **We broke our promises** | Promises in the offer that nobody checked or planned for | "Which card broke first, and which decision made it?" |
| **We kept the dates and hid the freeze** | The promises held, but Sarah learned the truth from someone else | "What does keeping quiet cost, even when the dates hold?" |
| **We kept our promises, and paid for it** | The word held, but the price or the team's weekends paid for it | "Where did the cost come from on the calendar?" |
| **We kept our promises, and it paid** | Understood, traded, planned | "Which early choice made the rest possible?" |

Encourage a second run with one choice changed. The cause-and-effect map makes it easy to see
what that one change did.

## Close with one commitment

The final screen offers a short personal action plan. Ask each person to write one sentence:
*"Before I promise a client anything next month, I will check it with…"*. The notes stay in
their browser and are never sent to the LMS.

## Evidence and limits

- The design follows published research on learning and game design, listed in `STRATEGY.md`.
  It has not yet been evaluated with employees. The timings and the "about a fifth" figure come
  from the literature, not from this game.
- Run `docs/LEARNER-EVALUATION.md` with a pilot group before a wide release, and record the
  results in `RELEASE-VERIFICATION.md`.
